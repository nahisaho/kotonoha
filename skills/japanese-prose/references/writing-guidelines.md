# Japanese prose writing guidelines

Use these rules while drafting or rewriting. They are constraints for
judgment, not search-and-replace recipes.

## Lead with the reader's required conclusion

State the decision, action, result, or answer before the background that
supports it. An introductory sentence must earn its place by changing what
the reader understands or does.

## Keep one main relationship per sentence

A sentence may contain qualifications, but its subject, predicate, and main
object must remain visible. Split a sentence when the reader must retain one
unfinished relationship while parsing another.

## Prefer explicit relationships over compressed noun chains

Long noun sequences hide whether terms express ownership, purpose, target, or
sequence. Add particles or a predicate when GiNZA identifies five or more
consecutive nouns.

## Use concrete actors and actions

Keep the sourced actor, recipient, and object attached to their actions.
Avoid turning actions into abstract nouns when a direct verb is clearer,
but do not supply an actor or motive that the source does not establish.
An objective nonhuman subject is normal in technical prose: a system can
require authentication or return a result without implying a human feeling.

For each changed sentence, trace its subject, predicate, modifiers, and
referents. If a pronoun or omitted actor cannot be resolved from the source
and nearby context, retain the ambiguity and ask the author rather than
adding a plausible explanation to reader-facing prose.

## Review imagery without narrowing the claim

Dependency-based personification and metaphor hints are a limited set of
review targets. Keep technical meanings, quotations, and deliberate imagery.
An optional literal rewrite must preserve what acts on what; it must not
substitute a specific action, outcome, or cause for an unresolved metaphor.

## Treat formulaic expressions as review targets

Expressions such as 「と言えるでしょう」 or 「以下のとおりです」 are not
automatically wrong. Keep them only when their rhetorical function is
necessary. Otherwise state the evidence, conclusion, or preview directly.
Do not delete a contrast, qualification, or preface merely to shorten the
text if it changes the claim's conditions or intended emphasis.

## Vary rhythm according to information weight

Do not force every sentence or paragraph to the same length. Use a short
sentence for a decisive result. Use a longer sentence only when its
relationships remain clear.

## Explain terminology at first use

Introduce an unfamiliar term by function before or beside its name. Preserve
established product names, protocol names, identifiers, and domain terms.

## Preserve uncertainty accurately

Keep distinctions among confirmed facts, estimates, assumptions, and
recommendations. Do not make prose smoother by increasing certainty.

## Preserve the function and state of the sentence

A request is not an observation, permission is not performance, and a plan
is not completed work. Keep these distinctions, including hearsay and
uncertainty, when changing endings. Use the document's appropriate style;
do not rotate endings just to make them look varied.

## Use emphasis sparingly and safely

Emphasize only the phrase that changes the reader's decision. In Markdown,
separate `**strong emphasis**` from adjacent prose with half-width spaces.

## End with the consequence

Close a section with what the evidence means for the reader's next decision
or action. Do not append a generic summary when the consequence is already
clear.
